Observations on Cabinet Secretary and Principal Secretary Vetting under the 13th Parliament

Working notes compiled 24 September 2026 from parliamentary records and press coverage (The Star, Capital FM, Parliament of Kenya releases). For internal use - verify against the Kenya Gazette and Hansard before external citation.

1. One rejection across three vetting cycles

Across three cabinet vetting cycles in this Parliament (September 2022: all 22 approved; August 2024: 19 of 20 approved; plus the Principal Secretary batch of 51 in November 2022, all approved), exactly one nominee has been rejected: Ms. Stella Soi Lang'at, nominee Cabinet Secretary for Gender, Culture, the Arts and Heritage (August 2024). The committee's stated ground: she "failed to demonstrate adequate knowledge of topical, administrative and technical issues touching on the portfolio." The Gender docket then sat vacant for roughly seven months until filled in March 2025. A single rejection in three cycles is the documented exception that maps the rule: parliamentary vetting of cabinet and PS nominees currently functions as confirmation, with scrutiny the rare deviation.

2. The 2024 returnee rate: dissolution without turnover of personnel

The July 2024 cabinet dissolution appeared to be a mass accountability event: 21 of 22 Cabinet Secretaries removed at once. Yet when the cabinet was reconstituted a month later, six of the nineteen approved nominees (32%) had served in the dissolved cabinet - several merely rotated between dockets (Duale from Defence to Environment, Chirchir from Energy to Roads, Murkomen from Roads to Youth, Tuya from Environment to Defence, Mvurya from Mining to Trade, Miano from EAC to Tourism). A protest-forced dissolution that returns a third of its personnel within weeks is a measurable data point for the "re-shuffling the same names" critique - and exactly the kind of pattern that only becomes visible when appointment histories are assembled in one place.

3. A nominee who declined, skipped vetting - and was appointed anyway

In the 2024 diplomatic cycle, former West Mugirango MP Vincent Mogaka Kemosi, nominated High Commissioner to Ghana, wrote to the Defence, Intelligence and Foreign Relations Committee declining the nomination (citing "compelling personal and family matters") and never appeared for vetting. He was nonetheless gazetted as ambassador to Accra in May 2024. The Public Appointments (Parliamentary Approval) Act requires hearings and House approval but does not bar approval of a nominee who fails to appear; the committee forwarded the name regardless. The structural lesson: the vetting gate only closes on nominees who walk through it. A public record that tracks who appeared, who did not, and what happened to the no-shows would make this gap visible automatically - it is currently nearly impossible to assemble after the fact.

4. Goma: the one station with demonstrated pushback

The Consul-General post for Goma, DR Congo has twice drawn substantive scrutiny - committee members reminded a 2025 nominee that "we have rejected a nominee to this station before for lack of knowledge." This is the only posting in the compiled record where knowledge-of-docket concerns have materially shaped vetting outcomes more than once. It suggests committee scrutiny is not uniformly absent but concentrated: where members see acute risk (a volatile posting), substantive questioning appears. The pattern - scrutiny as exception, confirmation as default - is itself a finding.

5. Soft-landing pathways out of cabinet

Dismissal from cabinet is not exit from public life; it is frequently a lateral move to another public office. Compiled examples: Florence Bore (Labour CS, dismissed July 2024; High Commissioner to Namibia, approved September 2025); Margaret Nyambura Ndung'u and Andrew Karanja (removed January 2025; nominated as envoys to Ghana and Brazil respectively - Ndung'u publicly declined). Justin Muturi's path ran the other direction (Attorney General in 2022 cabinet, then Public Service CS, then dismissed March 2025 after disagreeing publicly with the President). Exit pathways are part of the accountability record: if a removed official re-enters through appointment, the earlier record should resurface with them.

6. The impeachment-to-appointment pipeline

The Deputy President change in late 2024 (Gachagua impeached October-November 2024; Kindiki sworn in 1 November 2024) moved a parliamentary-vetted Cabinet Secretary into the second-highest office without a public approval hearing equivalent to what a CS nomination requires. This is not illegal - the constitution's DP succession path differs from Article 152 cabinet vetting - but it is a gap between the rigor applied to appointments and the rigor applied to elevations, and it belongs in any complete picture of how public offices are filled in this Parliament.

7. What this means for the vetting record

Taken together, the compiled record supports four design requirements for a durable vetting record: (1) track appearances, not just outcomes - who was nominated, who appeared, who was approved, who declined, who was rejected, and on what stated grounds; (2) track personnel across cycles - the returnee rate (32% in 2024) and docket rotations are only visible when appointment histories are linked per person; (3) track exit pathways - removal from office followed by re-appointment elsewhere is a continuity fact, not a fresh start; (4) note the stated grounds verbatim - the committee's own language ("failed to demonstrate adequate knowledge...") is the primary evidence of where scrutiny happened and where it did not.
